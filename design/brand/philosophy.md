# Reconciled Lines

_A design philosophy, written with the `canvas-design` skill before drawing the Tickline mark._

Reconciled Lines begins from the quietest objects in any office: the ruled line and the small mark
someone makes beside it after checking. A line is a promise that something was written; a mark
across it is proof that someone looked. The movement treats these two gestures — the horizontal
rule and the decisive stroke that crosses it — as a complete visual language. Nothing ornamental
survives. What remains must be meticulously crafted, every terminal and every angle the product of
painstaking adjustment, so that the simplest possible drawing carries the full weight of trust.

Space is organised as a ledger is: a strict underlying grid, generous margins, and long horizontal
calm interrupted by one deliberate diagonal. The diagonal is the only event. It earns its place by
crossing — never touching tangentially, never stopping short — and where it crosses, the line
beneath steps aside by a hair's breadth, the way ink leaves paper bare where a pen has lifted.
These knockouts are measured, not guessed; the work should feel like it was refined across countless
small revisions until each gap is exactly as wide as the eye expects.

Colour is a matter of ink, not decoration. A near-black for what was written, a quiet grey for what
is structural, and one deep sea-green reserved for the act of verification. The green never
appears as a field or a background; it is only ever a line. Surfaces stay pale and cool, the colour
of paper under office light. Restraint is the expertise here: a master-level palette is one where a
second accent would feel like a lie.

Scale is tested, never assumed. Every form must survive reduction to the size of a fingernail and
enlargement to the size of a wall without changing character. Stroke weights are tuned so the
diagonal stays legible when everything else has dissolved into a few pixels. The study of a form at
many sizes is part of the work, presented with the clinical seriousness of a specimen sheet —
the evidence that the form was examined as carefully as it was drawn.

Text is sparse and subordinate: small monospaced labels, figure numbers, measurements — the
annotation of a careful examiner rather than a headline. Words name what the drawing already shows.
The whole composition should read as a page from a well-kept technical record, laboured over with
deep expertise, in which the most important thing is also the smallest: one stroke, placed exactly.
